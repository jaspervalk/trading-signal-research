# Supply-constraint screener — methodology

**Status:** Layer A + B + C shipped (2026-08-06/07), branch `feat/supply-constraint-screener`.
**Code:** [`src/app/supply/`](../src/app/supply/) (`edgar.py`, `fundamentals.py`, `metrics.py`, `screen.py`, `constraints.py`, `layer_b.py`, `triggers.py`, `concentration.py`, `cache.py`), [`apps/api/app/routes/supply.py`](../apps/api/app/routes/supply.py), [`apps/web/src/app/supply/page.tsx`](../apps/web/src/app/supply/page.tsx).
**Config:** [`configs/supply_screen.yaml`](../configs/supply_screen.yaml) (Layer A thresholds), [`configs/supply_constraints.yaml`](../configs/supply_constraints.yaml) (Layer B registry), [`configs/supply_universe.csv`](../configs/supply_universe.csv) (which tickers get screened).

This is the primer for future-you. Read it before touching thresholds, before adding a constraint entry, and before trusting a number on the `/supply` page.

## 1. What the page is for

The pattern this screens for: a company with a fixed cost base and depressed gross margins, operating in a market whose supply is heading into deficit. When the deficit forces prices up, or lets the company stop discounting, the fixed costs don't move — so margin recovers fast and most of the recovered gross profit falls straight to the bottom line. NAND flash in 2024–2025 is the validating precedent: a ~4–5% supply deficit, according to the project owner's own review, coincided with a 234% price move in NAND-exposed names (`WDC`, `MU`). That's a historical data point cited to justify why this mechanism is worth screening for, not a claim this codebase has independently verified against an industry source — see the constraint registry entry for the caveats.

The inversion that makes this screen buildable at all: **the screener does not find shortages.** Nothing in EDGAR, yfinance, or any other feed this project touches says "titanium dioxide supply is tightening." The owner finds shortages — by reading trade press, company filings, capacity-closure announcements — and the screener finds who is financially exposed to whatever the owner already believes about the shortage. This is why the pipeline is split into two independent halves that never blend into a black-box score:

- **Layer A** (`app.supply.metrics`, `app.supply.screen`) is the financial side, fully mechanical: for every ticker in the universe, how far is the current gross margin below its own historical peak, how big would the recovery be against revenue and market cap, and can the business survive long enough to see it. Pure EDGAR + yfinance arithmetic — no LLM, no news, no opinion about any market.
- **Layer B** (`app.supply.constraints`, `app.supply.layer_b`) is the supply side, entirely hand-curated: a small, dated, sourced registry of specific markets the owner has personally researched and believes are supply-constrained, each with a list of tickers and how exposed their revenue is to that market.

Layer A can run over hundreds of tickers with zero domain knowledge and surface candidates nobody was already watching. Layer B can never be that broad — it only covers markets the owner has actually looked into — but it's what turns "this company has margin headroom" into "this company has margin headroom that a named, sourced supply constraint is withholding." A ticker with great Layer A metrics but no entry in the constraint registry just has margin headroom for some unknown reason; that's still worth a look, but it isn't the thesis this page is built to surface.

Layer C (`app.supply.triggers`) answers a third, separate question: has the compression actually started reversing yet, or is this still a thesis about the future.

No price data appears anywhere on this page — deliberately. This is a fundamentals screen; price action was called out as a distraction on it. Nothing here is a price target, an entry price, or a recommendation, as the page header itself says.

## 2. Reading the ranked watchlist

Every row is one ticker's Layer A result. Every score component is its own column — nothing is blended into a single number you have to trust blindly.

| Column | Meaning | Threshold (`configs/supply_screen.yaml`) |
|---|---|---|
| Torque | `earnings_torque` — see below, the headline metric | `>= 0.25` |
| GM %ile | `gm_percentile` — share of the ticker's own historical quarterly gross margins that sit *below* the current TTM gross margin. Low = margins are depressed relative to history. | `<= 0.40` |
| Headroom pp | `margin_headroom_pp` = `max(historical quarterly GM) − current TTM GM`, in percentage points | `>= 10.0` pp |
| Cap intensity | `capital_intensity` = PP&E / TTM revenue. High = fixed-cost-heavy, which is exactly the operating leverage this screen wants (costs don't rise when a margin recovery hits). | `>= 0.5` |
| Survival (q) | `survivability_quarters` = `(cash + max(TTM operating cash flow, 0)) / mean(negative quarterly operating cash flows)`. `∞` when the company has no negative OCF quarters — cash-generative, so "quarters until it runs out of cash" doesn't apply. | `>= 8` quarters (`∞` always clears) |
| History (q) | `quarters_of_history` — how many quarters of EDGAR margin history back every percentile/headroom/torque figure on the row | `>= 24` quarters (6 years), or every history-dependent field is `None` |
| Analysts | `numberOfAnalystOpinions` from yfinance | not a gate — see below |
| Coverage× | `coverage_multiplier` — ranking bonus for thin analyst coverage | reference point `12`; capped `[0.5, 2.0]` |

**`earnings_torque` is the headline metric, and it deserves the careful reading:** `earnings_torque = margin_headroom_fraction × TTM revenue / market cap`. Read it as: *if this company's margins returned to its own historical peak on today's revenue, how much incremental annual gross profit is that, expressed as a fraction of today's market cap.* A torque of `0.27` (Westlake's, currently the only row that passes every Layer A gate) means a margin recovery alone would add gross profit equal to 27% of the entire market cap in a single year — nothing else has to happen, no multiple expansion, no revenue growth. The `>= 0.25` threshold means: **above 0.25, margin recovery alone adds roughly a quarter of market cap in annual gross profit.** That is the size of opportunity Layer A is built to surface.

Analyst coverage is deliberately *not* a pass/fail gate — it used to be, and it excluded Westlake (`WLK`) outright for having 15 analysts covering it despite qualifying on every fundamental criterion (22.6pp headroom, strong torque, ample runway). Thin coverage is a *discovery* signal — fewer people looking is what makes a name interesting, not what makes the business good — so it now only affects ranking, via `coverage_multiplier`, folded into the Layer B score. A missing analyst count is recorded as a caveat on the row (uncovered, or just missing data) and never fails the screen either.

**Why the ranking is tiered, not sorted by torque alone.** `earnings_torque` divides by market cap, and a company in real financial distress has a compressed market cap — so distress mechanically inflates its own torque. Sorting by raw torque therefore systematically promotes companies that cannot survive long enough to benefit from the recovery the screen is trying to measure. This isn't hypothetical: as of the last run against `configs/supply_universe.csv`, `TROX` (Tronox, a pure-play TiO2 producer) has by far the highest torque in the entire 107-ticker universe — **0.79**, three times the passing threshold — but fails on survivability alone (5.6 quarters of runway against an 8-quarter floor). Sorted by torque alone it would rank #1. In the actual ranking it sits at **#69 of 107**, because the sort key is tiered:

1. **Passed** — cleared every Layer A gate.
2. **Near-miss, other reason** — has metrics, failed, but not on survivability alone (also covers insufficient-history rows, which can't even be evaluated for survivability).
3. **Near-miss, survivability only** — the *sole* failing reason is the runway floor. Demoted below tier 1's other near-misses on purpose: a company that can't outlast the cycle is disqualified in kind, not in degree.
4. **Unscored** — no CIK, fetch failure, or too little history to even build a TTM figure.

Within a tier, ties break on `-earnings_torque`, then ticker A–Z. `WLK` — lower torque (0.27) but nothing wrong with it — outranks `TROX`'s 0.79 because it actually passed. This is a tiered *sort key*, not a composite score: every metric stays its own visible column, nothing is blended.

Click a row for its near-miss detail (every failing reason, not just the first one that hit) and to load its gross-margin chart in the candidate-detail section below.

## 3. `quarters_of_history` and `dropped_implausible`

Both are on screen because a percentile computed over truncated history is wrong *without looking wrong* — the number still prints, still sorts, still looks like every other row.

**`quarters_of_history`** is why the 24-quarter (6-year) floor exists at all, and why it's visible per row rather than just enforced silently. Below the floor, every history-dependent field (`gm_percentile`, `margin_headroom_pp`, `earnings_torque`, `gm_volatility_pp`) is `None`, and `passes()` reports a single explicit reason (`insufficient_history: N quarters (need >= 24)`) instead of comparing `None` against a threshold and quietly failing for the wrong reason.

**`dropped_implausible`** exists because of a real incident: an early run of this screen, against the S&P 500 + Nasdaq-100 universe before the universe was narrowed to sector targets, ranked **General Electric #1** on **265 percentage points of phantom headroom**, from a printed gross margin of **292%**. The cause: GE's Q4 filing is a 10-K, not a 10-Q. EDGAR's XBRL only carries a consolidated quarterly revenue fact from 10-Qs — a 10-K reports annual figures — so the 80–100 day duration filter that identifies "a quarter" in `quarterly_series` picked up a *segment*-scoped revenue value roughly an order of magnitude too small for that period, while gross profit resolved correctly against the full consolidated figure. Profit over a too-small revenue denominator produced a mathematically impossible 292% margin.

The fix, in `app.supply.fundamentals.build_margin_history`: a gross margin outside `(-1.0, 1.0]` is not a tunable threshold, it's an arithmetic impossibility (profit can't exceed revenue by that much), so those quarters are dropped rather than corrupting a percentile — and counted in `dropped_implausible` so a reader can see how much of a ticker's reported history was thrown out before trusting its `gm_percentile` or `margin_headroom_pp`. Where several facts cover the same period (a consolidated total tagged alongside a segment or corporate line in the same filing), the larger absolute value wins, since the consolidated total is by construction at least as large as any segment of it.

## 4. What the clustering does and does not prove

At the last run, four of the top five ranked rows — `WLK`, `KRO`, `CC`, `HUN` — share the same `end_market` label: **construction**. `KRO` and `CC` (Kronos Worldwide and Chemours) are TiO2 pigment producers; `HUN` (Huntsman) sells MDI; `WLK` (Westlake) sells PVC. On the surface that reads like three or four independent confirmations of one thesis. It isn't.

**The clustering is mechanically guaranteed, not independent corroboration.** `TROX`, `KRO`, and `CC` are pure or near-pure TiO2 pigment producers selling the same commodity into the same buyers at close to the same price — by construction, commodity producers selling the same input have correlated margins. If TiO2 pricing moves, their gross margins move together, because they're the same fact measured three times through three different tickers, not three different facts. Layer A found one thing and it shows up as three rows.

And the deeper point cuts across even that: `TROX`/`KRO`/`CC` (pigment), `HUN` (MDI), and `WLK` (PVC) are five different products in five different specialty-chemical sub-industries — different GICS sectors, different customers, different producers. What they share is not the product, it's the **end market**: all five sell into construction and housing. A margin recovery thesis built on "four names in my top five passed the screen" is really a single, undiversified bet on one macro variable — construction demand — wearing four tickers as a disguise.

This is exactly why the end-market concentration line exists (`app.supply.concentration`, the "N of top 5 share end market X" banner above the table). `end_market` (`configs/supply_universe.csv`) is deliberately coarser than `sector` on purpose — GICS sub-industry can't see this concentration, because pigment and PVC pipe *are* different sub-industries. `end_market` can. The concentration check is **reporting only**: it counts and displays, and it never reorders, weights, or filters the ranked table above it. If four names cluster on one macro bet, the fix is a human decision (position-size across them as one bet, not four), not a ranking change — baking a de-duplication rule into the sort would just be inventing a second implicit scoring model on top of the first.

## 5. The constraint registry

`configs/supply_constraints.yaml` (loaded by `app.supply.constraints.load_constraints`) is hand-curated, edited on a monthly cadence, and will stay that way. This is a deliberate refusal, not a backlog item: **asked to find supply shortages, a language model will always find one.** Feed an LLM "is market X supply-constrained" and it will construct a plausible-sounding yes with real-sounding numbers, because that's what a fluent answer to that question looks like — not because the market actually is constrained. The registry only grows by the owner personally reading a primary or trade-press source and writing the entry by hand. It changes about once a month, by a person, and the git diff of that edit — what changed, why, with what source — is the audit trail. A database table would hide that diff behind a migration or an `UPDATE`; `git log` on this one file *is* the review history.

**The dated-source requirement is enforced at load time, not downstream.** Every `Constraint.deficit_source` must contain a 4-digit publication year, checked by a Pydantic field validator; if it doesn't, `load_constraints` raises `ValueError` naming the offending entry and **the entire file fails to load** — not a warning, a hard failure. No source, no constraint: a supply-deficit number nobody can trace to a dated source must never reach a score.

**What `confidence: low` means**, concretely, using the real TiO2 entry (`tio2_pigment_2026_capacity_vs_chinese_supply`): the entry documents genuine, dated capacity destruction on the Western supply side — Tronox permanently closed its Botlek, Netherlands plant (90,000 tonnes) in March 2025 and its Fuzhou plant in January 2026; Venator went through bankruptcy and restructuring; Tronox pushed global price increases of 2–4% in Q1 2026 despite weak demand, an explicit volume-to-margin shift. That's a real, sourced supply-side thesis. But the *same* research turned up counter-evidence just as strong, recorded in the same entry's `counter_evidence` field rather than left out or quietly weighed away: Chinese TiO2 exports were up 40.43% year-on-year in June 2026 (16.56% across H1 2026), July 2026 Chinese prices fell RMB 1,900/tonne while producer inventories rose 23.26%, and Chinese titanium concentrate remains oversupplied against sluggish property-chain demand. Western capacity is closing *and* Chinese exports are filling the gap — that tension is unresolved, which is exactly why this entry carries `confidence: low` and a `deficit_pct` of `null` rather than an invented number. A constraint can document real capacity destruction with no credible *projected* deficit; Layer B (`app.supply.layer_b`) treats a null `deficit_pct` as "cannot score," not as zero — the row still appears with `score=None` and a reason, never silently dropped.

The reason this matters beyond TiO2 specifically: **capacity discipline without a demand shock is only half the pattern that made NAND work, and Layer A cannot tell the two apart.** A producer cutting capacity to defend price, with weak underlying demand, can just as easily see a competitor's exports backfill the gap — no deficit, no price move, no margin recovery, and Layer A's financial screen would have looked identical either way right up until the outcome diverged. That distinction — is this capacity discipline *plus* a real demand shock, or capacity discipline *alone* — only exists in Layer B, because it requires reading two competing trade-press signals against each other and making a judgment call. It is not a number EDGAR or yfinance has ever carried, and it never will be.

`Constraint.is_stale` is set (not read from YAML — computed at load time against `last_reviewed`) when an entry hasn't been re-checked in more than 180 days. A stale constraint is still loaded and still scored — this module doesn't drop it — but the flag travels onto every `LayerBResult` it produces so nothing downstream can present a six-month-old number as fresh without saying so.

## 6. The inflection trigger

Layer C (`app.supply.triggers.evaluate_gross_margin_inflection`) answers the timing question Layer A and B don't: has the compression actually started reversing, or is this still a thesis about the future. It runs on pure arithmetic over the same `MarginHistory.gross_margins` series Layer A already computed — no new data source.

The definition, verbatim: **two consecutive quarters of sequential (quarter-over-quarter) gross-margin expansion, immediately following a declining trend** — where "declining trend" means the 4 quarters immediately before the expansion streak began show a net decline (first quarter of that window higher than its last quarter; doesn't have to be monotonic every step in between). Statuses: `insufficient_history` (fewer than 6 quarters available — a "cannot evaluate," not a false negative), `not_armed` (evaluated, pattern absent), `armed` (1 expanding quarter — loaded, watching for the second), `firing` (exactly 2 expanding quarters following a validated decline — the pattern just completed), `confirmed` (3+ expanding quarters — held up beyond the minimum bar). Only `firing`/`confirmed` are meant to draw a reader's eye in the UI (`draws_attention`); `armed` stays quiet by design.

This implements exactly one of the four triggers the original brief called for. **Trigger 2 — price versus volume — is deliberately unbuilt, not merely deferred.** It requires unit sales volume, and EDGAR's XBRL `companyfacts` does not reliably carry it: most filers never tag a volume concept at all, unlike revenue, cost of revenue, or gross margin, which are near-universal. The tempting workaround — proxy volume as revenue divided by an assumed average selling price — would silently launder a made-up assumption into a number that *reads* as measured data on the page. The judgment call here is the same one `survivability_quarters=None` makes rather than guessing a burn-rate number: stating the gap plainly and leaving the trigger unimplemented beats faking it. Triggers 3 and 4 are out of scope entirely — not stubbed, not planned.

## 7. Operating it

**Re-run button.** "Re-run research" on the `/supply` page is a two-step confirm (arm, then confirm) that calls `POST /supply/screen/refresh`. It purges the entire EDGAR disk cache (`data/cache/edgar/`) and re-fetches `companyfacts` for every resolved ticker in the universe, sequentially — SEC-throttled at roughly 6.7 requests/second (`_MIN_INTERVAL_S = 0.15s`, comfortably under SEC's 10 req/s limit), so it costs low tens of seconds of pure throttle on a cold cache plus however long ~107 large JSON documents actually take to download — the UI's own copy says "several minutes." A `threading.Lock` on the API guards against two refreshes running at once; a refresh already in flight returns `409`, not a second SEC-hammering run stacked on top. There's no dollar cost — EDGAR and yfinance are both free, unauthenticated (beyond the required header) APIs — the cost is wall-clock time and being polite to SEC's rate limit.

**Caching.** Two independent 7-day-ish caches: EDGAR `companyfacts` under `data/cache/edgar/CIK{cik:010d}.json`, TTL 7 days (`app.supply.cache`, `DEFAULT_TTL_HOURS = 24*7` — filings don't change intraday, and a single company's `companyfacts` document can carry 600+ concepts, so this is what makes a 100+ ticker screen tolerable at all on repeat runs). Separately, yfinance market cap / analyst-count lookups are cached 24h under `data/cache/supply_yf/` (`app.cli._fetch_supply_market_data`). A plain `GET /supply/screen` always serves off whatever's cached; only the refresh endpoint purges.

**SEC access.** `SEC_USER_AGENT = "trading-signal-research jaspermvalk@gmail.com"` (`app.supply.edgar`) is required — SEC blocks anonymous or unidentified clients by IP — and is not something to remove or genericize. Don't parallelize EDGAR fetches: `_throttle()` is a module-global timestamp with no lock, so concurrent callers could each read a stale value and jointly exceed the rate limit; the sequential cost (~20s of pure throttle on a cold cache) isn't worth the correctness risk of drawing an IP block.

**Which config file for what:**

| File | Governs | Edit when |
|---|---|---|
| `configs/supply_screen.yaml` | Layer A pass/fail thresholds and the coverage-multiplier reference point | Never casually — see the standing rule below |
| `configs/supply_constraints.yaml` | Layer B's hand-curated constraint registry | Monthly re-check of each cited source, or a new dated, sourced thesis is ready to add |
| `configs/supply_universe.csv` | Which ~107 tickers Layer A screens at all | A new sector or seed ticker worth watching is identified |

Both the CLI and the dashboard read the universe from `universe_path` in `configs/supply_screen.yaml`, so `tsr supply-screen` and the page always screen the same list. Pass `--universe` only when you deliberately want a different one. (Until 2026-08-07 the CLI defaulted to the other screener's S&P 500 list while the API used this one, so the same screen gave two different answers depending on where you ran it.)

**Thresholds are pre-registered and only move with a logged reason.** This is written directly into the top of `configs/supply_screen.yaml` and is worth repeating here because it's the single easiest rule to quietly violate: tuning a threshold after seeing which candidates pass or fail is how a screen becomes an overfit story generator — a document that always confirms whatever you already believed, dressed up as data. If a threshold needs to change, the commit message says why, before-and-after, not "adjusted for better results."

## 8. Known limits

- **US filers only.** The whole pipeline runs on SEC EDGAR's XBRL `companyfacts` endpoint, which is deepest for US domestic filers on 10-Q/10-K cadence. Foreign private issuers file annual 20-F or 40-F reports instead of 10-Qs, with much sparser or absent quarterly XBRL tagging — the same duration-filter mechanism that makes GE's Q4 10-K look like a phantom-headroom bug (Section 3) means a foreign filer's EDGAR history is likely to look thin or broken in the same way, not just smaller.
- **History coverage is partial today.** As of the last run against `configs/supply_universe.csv`, only **61 of 107** tickers in the screened universe clear the 24-quarter history floor; the other 46 are excluded from `passes()` outright with an explicit `insufficient_history` reason rather than a guessed percentile.
- **`end_market` labels are hand-assigned and unverified.** `configs/supply_universe.csv`'s `source` column reads `seed` for every row — every `end_market` value was manually assigned when the universe was built, not derived from a filing or a verified taxonomy. Treat the concentration banner as directionally right, not audited.
- **Exposure percentages are placeholders.** Every `revenue_exposure_pct` currently in `configs/supply_constraints.yaml` carries an `exposure_source` string that says, explicitly, "UNVERIFIED estimate pending 10-K segment-level work." None of the current entries are backed by an actual filed segment disclosure yet — that's real follow-up work, not finished infrastructure.
